Sample learning module · 25 minutes · all colleagues

Using AI safely and well at work

Faizaan Saeed · built as a sample for an AI Technical Trainer application.

A short, practical module for colleagues in a retail business who have just been given an approved AI assistant. No jargon: what it's good at, what never goes into it, and how to ask it properly.

What this is: a self-paced version of a module I'd also run live. Switch to the Facilitator guide tab to see how the session runs in a room or on Teams. Examples and the "approved tool" are fictional; in a real programme they'd use the client's own policy and tools.

By the end you'll be able to

  1. Say three things AI assistants are good at and two things they get wrong.
  2. Decide whether information is safe to put into the approved AI tool.
  3. Turn a vague request into a prompt that gets a useful first answer.
  4. Check an AI answer before you use it.
1 · About 5 minutes

What it's good at, and what it gets wrong

✓ Good at

  • First drafts: emails, notices, rota messages
  • Summarising long documents or feedback
  • Rewording for a different audience or tone
  • Brainstorming ideas and checklists

✕ Gets wrong

  • Facts and figures: it can sound sure and still be wrong
  • Anything after its training date unless it's given the source
  • Company policy it hasn't been shown
  • Judgement calls about people

The rule that covers all of it: AI drafts, you decide. You're responsible for anything you send, whoever wrote the first version.

2 · About 7 minutes

Keep data safe: the traffic light

Green: fine in the approved tool. Public information, general questions, your own wording with no names in it.
Amber: approved tool only, and remove the details. Internal documents and sales figures that aren't personal. Never put them into a personal or free AI account.
Red: never. Customer or colleague personal data, payment details, passwords, health or HR information, anything marked confidential.

4 · About 5 minutes

Quick knowledge check

Facilitator guide (live or on Teams, 45 minutes)

For groups of 8–16. The self-paced version above covers the same objectives for people who can't attend.

TimeActivityNotes for the facilitator
0–5Welcome; "what have you already tried AI for?" in the chatSets the level of the room. Note any red-data examples to come back to, without naming anyone.
5–12Good at / gets wrong, with a live demoAsk the tool for last month's store sales; it can't know them. Point out how confident the wrong answer sounds.
12–22Traffic light: sort 8 cards in pairsDebrief the amber ones; they cause the most debate. Link to the data protection policy.
22–35Prompt makeover on a real task from their roleStore teams: rota message. Head office: summarise survey comments. Everyone shares one before/after.
35–42Check before you use it: spot three errors in an AI draftErrors planted: a wrong date, an invented policy, a tone problem.
42–45Knowledge check and one commitment each"One task I'll try this week". Mention the drop-in clinic and the AI champions channel.

Adapting for audiences

  • Store colleagues: phone-first, shorter, rota and customer-email examples.
  • Managers: add "reviewing your team's AI use" and when to escalate.
  • Technical teams: add data connectors, prompt patterns and output checking at scale.

Accessibility

  • Captions on, slides shared in advance, high-contrast examples.
  • Every activity has a verbal and a written way to take part.
  • The self-paced version works with a keyboard and a screen reader.

Measuring whether it worked

  • Reaction: 3-question feedback at the end.
  • Learning: knowledge-check scores before and after.
  • Behaviour: 30-day follow-up: are people using the approved tool, and for what?
  • Results: fewer red-data incidents; time saved on the tasks people named.

Keeping it current

  • Review every quarter, or when the tool or policy changes.
  • Champions clinics feed real questions back into the examples.
  • Version number and change log on the last slide.
