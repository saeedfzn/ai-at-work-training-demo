Using AI safely and well at work

A short, practical module for colleagues in a retail business who have just been given an approved AI assistant. No jargon: how it works, what never goes into it, how to ask it properly, and how to check what it gives you.

What this is: the self-paced version of a module I'd also run live. Examples and the "approved tool" are fictional; in a real programme they'd follow the client's own AI and data protection policies.

Quick start: what do you already know?

1 · About 3 minutes

How it works, and what it gets wrong

In one sentence: an AI assistant predicts the most likely next words based on patterns in the text it was trained on. It doesn't look facts up unless it's been connected to a source, so it can sound completely sure and still be wrong.

✓ Good at

  • First drafts: emails, notices, rota messages
  • Summarising long documents or feedback
  • Rewording for a different audience or tone
  • Brainstorming ideas and checklists

✕ Gets wrong

  • Facts and figures it wasn't given
  • Company policy it hasn't been shown: it may invent one
  • Anything recent, unless connected to a live source
  • Judgement calls about people

The rule that covers all of it: AI drafts, you decide. You're responsible for anything you send, whoever wrote the first version.

2 · About 4 minutes

Keep data safe: the traffic light

Green: fine in the approved tool. Public information, general questions, your own wording with no names in it.
Amber: approved tool only, minimum needed. Internal business information such as sales figures or plans. Use only what the task needs, and never put it into a personal or free AI account.
Red: not unless your policy says this tool is approved for it. Personal data about customers or colleagues, payment details, passwords, and especially special category data such as health information. When in doubt, leave it out and ask.

3 · About 2 minutes

Stay secure

AI makes scams more convincing

Phishing emails no longer have spelling mistakes, and voices can be faked. Check unusual requests for money, passwords or gift cards through a second channel, every time.

Only use approved tools

Fake "AI" apps and browser extensions are a common way to steal data. If it isn't on the approved list, don't install it or sign in to it with work details.

Pasted something you shouldn't have? Report it straight away to the service desk. It's fixable, and reporting quickly is what matters: no blame.

5 · About 2 minutes

Check before you use it: spot the three errors

A colleague asked the AI to draft a notice about the new returns window. Tap the three parts that are wrong.

Hi team, from Monday 31 November our returns window changes to 30 days. As per our 2025 policy, customers without a receipt get a full cash refund. Please let customers know at the till. Any staff who get this wrong will be disciplined. Thanks!

Adapting for audiences

  • Store colleagues: phone-first, 20 minutes, rota and customer-email examples.
  • Managers: add "reviewing your team's AI use" and when to escalate.
  • Technical teams: add data connectors, prompt patterns and output testing at scale.

Accessibility

  • Captions on, materials shared in advance, high-contrast examples.
  • Every activity has a spoken and a written way to take part.
  • The self-paced page is keyboard-navigable, with feedback announced to screen readers.

Measuring whether it worked

  • Reaction: 3 questions at the end (useful? confident? what's missing?).
  • Learning: quick-start vs knowledge-check scores.
  • Behaviour: 30-day follow-up and the tool's own usage data: are people using it, and for what?
  • Results: fewer data incidents, and time saved on the tasks people named.
  • Findings go into a monthly note with recommended changes to the content.

Keeping it current

Reviewed every quarter, or whenever the tool, the policy or the law changes. Questions from the champions clinics feed back into the examples. A version number and change log sit on the last page.
